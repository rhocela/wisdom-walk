# Day 90: Cities of Refuge and Faithful Promises

⏱️ *Estimated reading time: 25-28 minutes*

<ProgressTracker dayNumber={90} />

## Chronological Setting

The land division is nearly complete. Each tribe has received its inheritance, including Joshua himself, who modestly takes his portion last—the city of Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim, which he rebuilds and inhabits. Now God commands the establishment of **cities of refuge**—six strategically located cities where someone who accidentally kills another person can flee for protection from the "avenger of blood" (the victim's family member seeking vengeance). This system balances justice with mercy, protecting the innocent while ensuring accountability. The book of Joshua concludes its land distribution narrative with a powerful summary: God kept every promise He made. Not one word failed. The land God swore to give Abraham's descendants now belongs to Israel, and they dwell in it with rest from their enemies. This triumphant declaration celebrates God's covenant faithfulness across generations.

## Historical Context

**Joshua's Humble Inheritance (Joshua 19:49-51)**: After all the tribes receive their portions, the people give Joshua an inheritance "by command of the LORD"—the city of Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim. Joshua doesn't claim the best land or a strategic location. He waits until everyone else is settled, then receives a single city, which he rebuilds. This humility and servant leadership mirrors Moses, who led Israel to the Promised Land but didn't enter it himself. Joshua leads Israel into the land, distributes it to all the tribes, and only then receives his own portion. True leadership serves others first.

**Cities of Refuge Established (Joshua 20:1-9)**: God commands Joshua to designate six cities of refuge as originally instructed in Numbers 35. Three cities are west of the Jordan (Kedesh in Galilee, Shechem in Ephraim, Hebron in Judah) and three are east (Bezer in Reuben, Ramoth in Gad, Golan in Manasseh). These cities are strategically distributed so anyone in Israel can reach one quickly. The system works like this: if someone accidentally kills another person (for example, an ax head flies off and strikes someone), they can flee to a city of refuge. At the city gate, they present their case to the elders. If accepted, they dwell there safely until they can stand trial before the congregation. If found guilty of murder, they're handed over to the avenger of blood. If found innocent of intentional murder, they must remain in the city of refuge until the death of the high priest, after which they can return home without fear of vengeance.

This system reveals several profound truths: (1) **Life is sacred**—even accidental death requires reckoning; (2) **Justice requires process**—mob vengeance is replaced with orderly trial; (3) **Mercy tempers justice**—the unintentional killer receives protection; (4) **Access is universal**—cities are distributed so everyone can reach safety; (5) **The high priest's death brings freedom**—his death releases the refugee from exile.

**God's Complete Faithfulness (Joshua 21:43-45)**: After distributing the land and establishing the cities of refuge, the narrator pauses to make a monumental declaration: "Thus the LORD gave to Israel all the land that he swore to give to their fathers. And they took possession of it, and they settled there. And the LORD gave them rest on every side just as he had sworn to their fathers. Not one of all their enemies had withstood them, for the LORD had given all their enemies into their hands. Not one word of all the good promises that the LORD had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass."

This threefold repetition emphasizes God's faithfulness: (1) He gave the land He promised; (2) He gave rest from enemies as He promised; (3) Not one promise failed—**all came to pass**. What God swore to Abraham in Genesis 12, to Isaac in Genesis 26, to Jacob in Genesis 28, and to Moses in Exodus 6 has been fulfilled. Though the conquest is incomplete in some areas and challenges remain, the essential promise is kept—Israel possesses the land and dwells in it with rest.

**Eastern Tribes Dismissed (Joshua 22:1-9)**: Joshua summons the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh who had settled east of the Jordan. They had kept their promise to Moses (Numbers 32) by leaving their families and crossing the Jordan to fight alongside their brothers for seven years. Now Joshua blesses them, commends their faithfulness, and sends them home with this charge: "Only be very careful to observe the commandment and the law that Moses the servant of the LORD commanded you, to love the LORD your God, and to walk in all his ways and to keep his commandments and to cling to him and to serve him with all your heart and with all your soul" (22:5). They return home with great wealth to share with their families—the plunder from years of conquest.

## Redemptive Significance

**Christ Our Refuge**: The cities of refuge powerfully foreshadow **Jesus Christ as our refuge from judgment**. Just as the unintentional killer fled to a city of refuge for safety from the avenger of blood, sinners flee to Christ for safety from God's wrath against sin. Hebrews 6:18 describes believers as those "who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us"—the language explicitly echoes the cities of refuge. 

Several parallels illuminate this typology:
- **Access for all**: The cities were strategically located so anyone could reach them; Christ is accessible to all who call on Him—"whoever calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved" (Romans 10:13).
- **Immediate protection**: The refugee received protection upon entering the city; believers receive immediate justification upon trusting Christ.
- **The high priest's death**: The refugee gained permanent freedom when the high priest died; we gain permanent freedom because our High Priest, Jesus, died and rose again. His death doesn't just cover our exile—it ends it forever.
- **Intentional vs. unintentional**: The cities distinguished between murder and manslaughter; Christ's atonement covers all sin—intentional and unintentional—for those who trust Him.

**God's Promises Never Fail**: Joshua 21:45 declares, "Not one word of all the good promises that the LORD had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass." This becomes a foundation for trusting God's future promises. If God kept His covenant promises to Israel across 400+ years (from Abraham to Joshua), we can trust He will keep His promises to us. Peter writes, "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise" (2 Peter 3:9). What God promises in His word **will come to pass**—salvation, eternal life, resurrection, new creation, Christ's return, final judgment, new heavens and new earth. Not one word will fail.

**Servant Leadership**: Joshua's example of receiving his inheritance last, after serving everyone else, reflects Christ who "came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Jesus, though Lord of all, humbled Himself, became a servant, and will receive His full inheritance only after His bride, the church, is complete. True leadership is characterized by service, humility, and putting others first.

## Connecting the Dots

**Our Strong Refuge (Hebrews 6:17-20)**: The New Testament explicitly connects the Old Testament imagery of refuge to Christ. Hebrews 6:18 speaks of those "who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us." This hope, anchored in God's unchangeable promise and confirmed by His oath, is described as "a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul, a hope that enters into the inner place behind the curtain, where Jesus has gone as a forerunner on our behalf, having become a high priest forever" (6:19-20).

Notice the progression: we **flee for refuge** (like the manslayer fleeing to the city), we **lay hold of hope** (like grasping the city gate), and this hope **anchors our soul** (providing security), leading us **behind the curtain** (into God's presence) where **Jesus our High Priest** has already entered. The entire city of refuge system finds its fulfillment in Christ. He is not just *a* refuge—He is our **only** refuge from the wrath we deserve.

**God's Oath and Promises**: Hebrews 6:17 explains that God confirmed His promise with an oath "so that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to hold fast to the hope set before us." God's **word** is unchangeable (He cannot lie); God's **oath** is unchangeable (He swears by Himself). These two guarantees give us absolute certainty. Just as Joshua 21:45 declared that not one promise to Israel failed, Hebrews assures us that not one promise to believers will fail. Our salvation rests on God's character, not our performance.

**Love and Obedience**: Joshua's charge to the eastern tribes combines love and law: "love the LORD your God, and... walk in all his ways and... keep his commandments" (22:5). This anticipates Jesus' teaching: "If you love me, you will keep my commandments" (John 14:15). Obedience flows from love, not the reverse. We don't obey to earn God's love; we obey because we've received it. The eastern tribes' seven years of faithful service despite separation from their families demonstrates this love-motivated obedience.

## Theological Threads

- **Divine Faithfulness**: The repeated emphasis that "not one word" of God's promises failed (Joshua 21:45) establishes God's absolute reliability. His faithfulness doesn't depend on circumstances, human cooperation, or time delays. What God promises, God performs. This truth anchors our faith in all His promises—past, present, and future.

- **Justice and Mercy United**: The cities of refuge demonstrate that God's character holds justice and mercy in perfect balance. Justice demands accountability for life taken; mercy provides refuge for the unintentional killer. At the cross, these attributes meet perfectly—justice is fully satisfied through Christ's death; mercy is fully extended to all who believe.

- **Substitutionary Death**: The high priest's death releasing the refugee from exile beautifully foreshadows Christ's substitutionary atonement. The refugee didn't die; the high priest died, and the refugee went free. We don't die for our sins; Christ died, and we go free. His death is our liberation.

- **Accessible Grace**: The strategic placement of six cities throughout Israel ensured that refuge was always within reach. Similarly, God's grace in Christ is accessible to all—no geographical, ethnic, social, or economic barriers prevent anyone from fleeing to Christ for refuge.

- **Humble Service**: Joshua's willingness to receive his inheritance last, after ensuring everyone else was settled, models Christlike servant leadership. Greatness in God's kingdom is measured by service, not status; by giving, not getting; by putting others first, not asserting rights.

## Study Questions

1. **Fleeing for Refuge**: Hebrews 6:18 describes believers as those "who have fled for refuge" to Christ. Have you consciously fled to Jesus as your refuge from God's judgment? What does it mean practically to "lay hold" of the hope He offers?

2. **God's Unfailing Promises**: Joshua 21:45 declares, "Not one word of all the good promises that the LORD had made... had failed; all came to pass." What specific promises from God's Word do you need to trust more fully today? How does God's faithfulness to Israel strengthen your confidence in His promises to you?

3. **The High Priest's Death**: In the cities of refuge, the high priest's death released the refugee from exile. How does understanding Christ as your High Priest whose death has set you free forever deepen your appreciation for His sacrifice?

4. **Servant Leadership**: Joshua received his inheritance last, after serving everyone else. In what areas of your life is God calling you to practice servant leadership—putting others' needs ahead of your own comfort or advancement?

5. **Love and Obedience**: Joshua charged the eastern tribes to "love the LORD your God" and "keep his commandments" (22:5). How does genuine love for God motivate obedience differently than duty or fear? Which commandments do you find hardest to obey, and how can deeper love for God help?

6. **Anchor of Hope**: Hebrews 6:19 calls our hope in Christ "a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul." In what circumstances do you most need this anchor? How can you remind yourself in stormy times that your soul is anchored securely in Christ?
