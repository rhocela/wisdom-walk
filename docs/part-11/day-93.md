# Day 93: The Cycle Begins

⏱️ *Estimated reading time: 30-32 minutes*

<ProgressTracker dayNumber={93} />

## Chronological Setting

After the miraculous victory over the five southern kings and the defense of Gibeon, Joshua leads Israel in a systematic campaign to conquer the southern region of Canaan, taking city after city in rapid succession. Then a northern coalition forms under Jabin, king of Hazor, gathering a vast army "as numerous as the sand that is on the seashore" with horses and chariots—the ancient equivalent of modern armored divisions. Despite the overwhelming odds, the LORD tells Joshua, "Do not be afraid of them, for tomorrow at this time I will give over all of them, slain, to Israel." Joshua attacks suddenly, defeats the coalition completely, and burns Hazor to the ground. The conquest chapters conclude with a comprehensive summary: Joshua took the whole land, destroyed all the kings, left none remaining, and gave the land to Israel as an inheritance according to their tribal divisions. The land has rest from war—at least temporarily. Though pockets of resistance remain and the conquest is incomplete in some areas, the major military campaign is finished, and Israel begins the process of settling into the land God promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

## Historical Context

**The Southern Campaign (Joshua 10:16-43)**: After the defeat of the five-king coalition, Joshua pursues a relentless campaign through the south. The five kings hide in a cave at Makkedah; Joshua seals them in, completes the battle, then brings them out, has his commanders put their feet on the kings' necks (symbolizing total victory), and executes them. Then Joshua systematically conquers Makkedah, Libnah, Lachish, Eglon, Hebron, and Debir—striking each city, destroying it completely, and leaving no survivors. The text repeatedly emphasizes that Joshua "left none remaining" and "devoted to destruction all that breathed, just as the LORD God of Israel commanded."

Archaeological evidence confirms that many of these southern cities experienced violent destruction during this period, though the exact correlation with biblical accounts remains debated. The campaign is swift and comprehensive—Joshua takes "all these kings and their land at one time, because the LORD God of Israel fought for Israel" (Joshua 10:42).

**The Northern Coalition (Joshua 11:1-9)**: When Jabin, king of Hazor (the most powerful city in northern Canaan), hears of Israel's victories, he forms a massive coalition including kings from the northern hill country, the Arabah, the lowland, and the coast. They gather at the waters of Merom with "a great horde, in number like the sand that is on the seashore, with very many horses and chariots." This is the most formidable force Israel has faced—horses and chariots gave ancient armies tremendous tactical advantages.

But the LORD tells Joshua: "Do not be afraid of them, for tomorrow at this time I will give over all of them, slain, to Israel. You shall hamstring their horses and burn their chariots with fire." Joshua makes a surprise attack, the LORD throws the enemy into panic, and Israel pursues them in all directions. Joshua follows God's command precisely: hamstringing the horses (cutting their leg tendons to render them useless) and burning the chariots, ensuring Israel will not rely on military technology but on the LORD.

**Hazor Destroyed (Joshua 11:10-15)**: Joshua turns back and captures Hazor, the head of all the northern kingdoms. He strikes down its king and burns the city—the only northern city burned besides Ai. The text emphasizes that Joshua "did all that Moses the servant of the LORD had commanded" (v. 15), showing his faithful obedience to the Mosaic covenant.

**Summary of the Conquest (Joshua 11:16-23)**: The narrative provides a sweeping overview: "Joshua took all that land... all the kings... Joshua made war a long time with all those kings" (the campaign lasted several years). He destroyed the Anakim (the giants who terrified the spies forty years earlier) from the hill country. Only in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod did some Anakim remain (ironically, Goliath would later come from Gath). The summary declares: "Joshua took the whole land, according to all that the LORD had spoken to Moses. And Joshua gave it for an inheritance to Israel according to their tribal allotments. And the land had rest from war."

**The King List (Joshua 12:1-24)**: This chapter catalogs all the kings defeated—first the two Transjordan kings defeated under Moses (Sihon and Og), then thirty-one kings west of the Jordan defeated under Joshua. This precise record serves as a permanent testimony to God's faithfulness in giving Israel the land.

## Redemptive Significance

**God's Promise Fulfilled**: The conquest represents the culmination of promises made centuries earlier. God told Abraham, "To your offspring I will give this land" (Genesis 12:7). He told Moses, "I will give it to you for a possession" (Exodus 6:8). Now, under Joshua, the promise is fulfilled: "The LORD gave to Israel all the land that he swore to give to their fathers... Not one word of all the good promises that the LORD had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass" (Joshua 21:43, 45). **God keeps His promises**—even when fulfillment takes centuries.

**Divine Warfare**: The conquest was not merely human warfare but divine judgment executed through Israel as God's instrument. The Canaanites' sin had "reached its full measure" (Genesis 15:16). Their religious practices included child sacrifice, temple prostitution, and abominable idolatry. **God's judgment on sin is real and severe**, whether executed through Israel's swords or through other means. This anticipates the ultimate judgment when Christ returns to judge the living and the dead.

**Incomplete Victory**: Though Joshua "took the whole land," later chapters reveal that significant pockets of Canaanite resistance remained. The book of Judges shows Israel's failure to complete the conquest and the resulting spiritual compromise. This illustrates a spiritual truth: **God gives victory, but His people must appropriate it fully**. Partial obedience leads to ongoing struggle.

**Rest from War**: The phrase "the land had rest from war" appears multiple times (Joshua 11:23; 14:15). This rest, however, is temporary and conditional on Israel's obedience. It points forward to the ultimate rest found in Christ. Hebrews 4 explains that Joshua did not give Israel true rest—that rest remains for God's people, entered through faith in Jesus Christ.

## Connecting the Dots

**The Greater Conquest**: Joshua's military conquest of Canaan foreshadows Christ's spiritual conquest of sin, death, and Satan. Just as Joshua defeated enemy kings and gave Israel rest in the land, Jesus defeated spiritual enemies and gives believers rest in God. **Christ is the greater Joshua** who leads His people into the true Promised Land—eternal life and new creation.

**The Rest That Remains**: Hebrews 4 explicitly connects Joshua's conquest to the Christian life. Though Joshua gave Israel temporal rest from enemies, "there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9). This rest is entered by faith in Christ's finished work. **We cease from our striving** and rest in His completed redemption, just as God rested from His completed creation work.

**The King List and Christ's Victory**: Joshua 12's catalog of thirty-one defeated kings points to Christ's comprehensive victory over all spiritual enemies. Colossians 2:15 declares that Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him." Just as Joshua defeated every king, **Christ defeats every enemy** that opposes God's people.

**The Command to Destroy**: The complete destruction (herem) commanded in Joshua troubles many modern readers. Several points help us understand this in light of Christ:

1. **Judgment, not ethnic cleansing**: God's command targeted the Canaanites' sin and idolatry, not their ethnicity. Rahab and the Gibeonites, who turned to Israel's God, were spared.

2. **Temporary, not permanent**: This was a unique, unrepeatable event in redemptive history, not a pattern for the church. Christ commands us to love enemies, not destroy them (Matthew 5:44).

3. **Foreshadowing spiritual warfare**: The physical conquest pictures the spiritual warfare believers wage against sin, Satan, and worldly systems—not against flesh and blood (Ephesians 6:12).

4. **Pointing to final judgment**: The conquest previews the final judgment when Christ will utterly destroy all evil and wickedness forever.

## Theological Threads

- **God's Faithfulness**: The repeated emphasis that "not one word" of God's promises failed demonstrates His absolute faithfulness. What God promises, He performs. This gives believers confidence that His promises to us in Christ will likewise be fulfilled completely.

- **Human Responsibility and Divine Sovereignty**: God promised to give Israel the land, yet they had to fight for it. This balance appears throughout Scripture: God is sovereign and accomplishes His purposes, yet He works through human obedience and effort. **We fight because victory is certain, not to achieve uncertain victory**.

- **The Seriousness of Sin**: The total destruction of the Canaanite cities reveals sin's exceeding sinfulness. God does not wink at wickedness. This should drive us to the cross in gratitude, knowing that the judgment we deserved fell on Christ.

- **Incomplete Obedience**: Israel's failure to complete the conquest leads to centuries of spiritual compromise. This warns us that **partial obedience is still disobedience**. We must "take every thought captive to obey Christ" (2 Corinthians 10:5), leaving no area of life unconquered by His lordship.

## Study Questions

1. **God's Promises Fulfilled**: Joshua 11:23 says Joshua took the whole land "according to all that the LORD had spoken to Moses." How does seeing God's faithfulness in fulfilling centuries-old promises strengthen your faith in His promises to you in Christ?

2. **Divine Warfare**: The conquest involved God fighting for Israel against the Canaanite nations. What spiritual enemies does God fight for you as a believer? How can you trust Him to give you victory over sin, temptation, and spiritual opposition?

3. **Fear and Faith**: God told Joshua, "Do not be afraid" when facing the massive northern coalition (Joshua 11:6). What overwhelming circumstances in your life require you to trust God's promise of victory despite appearances?

4. **Incomplete Victory**: Though Joshua conquered the land, later chapters reveal incomplete obedience and remaining enemies. What areas of your life remain "unconquered"—places where you've not fully surrendered to Christ's lordship? What keeps you from complete obedience?

5. **The Rest That Remains**: Hebrews 4 says there remains a Sabbath rest for God's people, entered by faith. Are you striving in your own strength, or are you resting in Christ's finished work? What would it look like to truly "cease from your labors" and rest in His completed redemption?

6. **The King List**: Joshua 12 lists thirty-one conquered kings as a testimony to God's victory. If you were to list the "defeated enemies" in your spiritual life—sins conquered, fears overcome, strongholds broken—what would be on your list? How does remembering past victories encourage you for present battles?
