# Day 78: Look and Live

⏱️ *Estimated reading time: 32-34 minutes*

<ProgressTracker dayNumber={78} />

## Chronological Setting

Nearly forty years have passed since Israel's rebellion at Kadesh Barnea. The generation that refused to trust God and enter the Promised Land is dying in the wilderness. Now, as the nation returns to the **Kadesh region**, they face both the end of an era (Aaron's death) and a crisis that will produce one of Scripture's most powerful symbols of salvation—the **bronze serpent** lifted up in the wilderness. Israel is on the move again, circling around Edom and approaching the Promised Land from the east. Their journey is marked by both judgment and deliverance, rebellion and redemption.

## Historical Context

**Numbers 20:14-29** records a significant diplomatic failure and a generational transition. Moses sent messengers to the **king of Edom** (descendants of Esau, Jacob's brother) requesting passage through their territory along the King's Highway. Despite Israel's assurances that they would stay on the road, pay for any water used, and cause no trouble, **Edom refused** and threatened military force. Rather than fight their relatives, Israel took a long detour around Edom's territory.

At **Mount Hor**, on the border of Edom, God commanded Moses to bring Aaron and his son **Eleazar** up the mountain. There, Moses removed Aaron's high priestly garments and placed them on Eleazar, formally transferring the high priesthood to the next generation. **Aaron died** on the mountain at age 123, having served as high priest for nearly forty years. The entire congregation mourned him for thirty days. Aaron's death marked the passing of the exodus generation's leadership—Moses would follow soon after.

**Numbers 21** opens with Israel's first military victory in nearly four decades. The **Canaanite king of Arad** attacked Israel but was utterly defeated after Israel made a vow to the Lord. This victory contrasted sharply with the presumptuous defeat recorded in Numbers 14:39-45, demonstrating that success comes from God's blessing, not human confidence.

Yet even after this victory, **the people complained again**. Weary of the long journey around Edom, they spoke against God and Moses: "Why have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no food and no water, and we loathe this worthless food" (Numbers 21:5). Their complaint was remarkably similar to earlier grumblings, showing that even decades of wilderness wandering hadn't cured their propensity toward ingratitude.

God's judgment was swift: **fiery serpents** (possibly called "fiery" due to the burning pain of their venom) invaded the camp. Many Israelites died from snakebites. The people confessed their sin and begged Moses to intercede. God instructed Moses to make a **bronze serpent** and set it on a pole. Anyone bitten who looked at the bronze serpent would live.

The symbolism is striking: **salvation came not through fighting the serpents or applying medicine, but through simple faith—looking at what God provided**. The bronze serpent represented the judgment (shaped like a serpent) yet provided healing. Those who refused to look perished; those who looked lived, regardless of how serious their bite.

After this crisis, Israel resumed their journey and experienced remarkable victories over **King Sihon of the Amorites** and **King Og of Bashan**. These weren't diplomatic negotiations like with Edom but full-scale battles. Both kings refused Israel passage and attacked them. Both were utterly defeated, and Israel took possession of their lands east of the Jordan River. These territories would eventually become the inheritance of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh.

## Redemptive Significance

The **bronze serpent** stands as one of the Old Testament's clearest types of Christ's crucifixion. Jesus Himself made the connection explicit in His conversation with Nicodemus: **"And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life"** (John 3:14-15).

Consider the parallels:
- **The Problem**: Deadly snakebites (sin's deadly poison)
- **The Source**: Israel's rebellion invited judgment (our sin brings death)
- **The Remedy**: God-provided, not self-achieved (salvation by grace)
- **The Method**: A serpent lifted up (Christ became sin for us, 2 Corinthians 5:21)
- **The Response**: Looking in faith (believing in Christ)
- **The Result**: Life for the dying (eternal life for the spiritually dead)

The bronze serpent didn't heal by magic or medicine but by **faith's obedient response to God's promise**. Similarly, salvation doesn't come through moral improvement or religious ritual but through **looking to Christ crucified** in faith. The cure seems too simple—just look?—yet that's precisely the point. Salvation is **God's work received by faith**, not human work achieved by effort.

Tragically, this bronze serpent later became an object of idolatry. By King Hezekiah's time (about 700 years later), Israelites were burning incense to it. Hezekiah destroyed it, calling it "Nehushtan" (a piece of bronze, 2 Kings 18:4). Even God-ordained symbols can become idols when we worship the symbol rather than the reality it represents.

**Aaron's death** on Mount Hor marks the transition from the exodus generation to the conquest generation. Aaron, who made the golden calf yet received mercy, who interceded to stop plagues, whose staff budded to vindicate God's choice, now passes his office to Eleazar. The high priesthood continues unbroken, pointing forward to Christ, our eternal High Priest who "continues forever" (Hebrews 7:24).

**Edom's refusal** to allow passage demonstrates the lasting effects of old family conflicts (Jacob and Esau). Despite Israel's peaceful request and family connection, Edom chose hostility. This sets a pattern: Edom will remain Israel's enemy throughout Scripture, eventually representing God's enemies in prophetic literature (Obadiah, Malachi 1:2-5).

The **victories over Sihon and Og** show God fulfilling His promise to give Israel the land. These weren't easy opponents—Og was described as the last of the Rephaim (giants), and his iron bed measured 13.5 feet long (Deuteronomy 3:11). Yet Israel, empowered by God, defeated them completely. These victories encouraged the nation that the God who gave them Sihon and Og's kingdoms could give them Canaan itself.

## Connecting the Dots

**John 3:14-21** contains Jesus' direct application of the bronze serpent event to His own mission. In His nighttime conversation with Nicodemus, a respected teacher of Israel, Jesus explained that just as those dying from snakebites found life by looking at the lifted serpent, so those dying in sin find eternal life by believing in the lifted Son of Man.

The passage emphasizes several crucial truths:
- **God's love motivated salvation**: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son" (John 3:16)
- **Belief is the requirement**: "whoever believes in him" (repeated three times in John 3:15-18)
- **The alternative is perishing**: Those who don't believe "are condemned already" (John 3:18)
- **Light exposes darkness**: People reject Christ because they love darkness rather than light (John 3:19-20)

Just as Israelites had to **actively look** at the bronze serpent (not just know about it or admire it from a distance), so we must actively **believe in Christ**—trusting Him personally for salvation, not merely acknowledging historical facts about Him.

**1 Corinthians 10:14-22** returns to Paul's warnings about Israel's wilderness failures. Having discussed their idolatry, sexual immorality, testing Christ, and grumbling in the earlier verses (1 Corinthians 10:1-13, which we read in Day 76), Paul now applies these lessons: **"Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry"** (1 Corinthians 10:14).

Paul argues that participating in pagan worship is incompatible with participating in Christ's table. You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot partake of the table of the Lord and the table of demons. The issue isn't whether idols are real (they're not) but whether fellowship with demons is real (it is). Just as Israel's bronze serpent eventually became an idol requiring destruction, Christians must guard against allowing good things (even religious practices) to become substitutes for genuine worship of God.

The connection to Numbers 21 is the persistent danger of **spiritual unfaithfulness**. Israel complained despite God's provision, inviting judgment. Christians who presume on grace while living in compromise invite spiritual danger. The cure—looking to Christ in faith—must remain our constant practice, not a one-time decision.

## Theological Threads

- **Salvation by Looking/Believing**: The bronze serpent incident illustrates that salvation comes through faith, not works. Israelites didn't earn healing by fighting snakes, offering sacrifices, or proving worthiness. They simply looked in faith at what God provided. This foreshadows salvation through faith in Christ crucified. Human pride resists such simplicity, preferring to contribute to salvation, but God's way is: "Look and live."

- **Christ Bearing Our Curse**: The bronze serpent represented the thing that brought death (a serpent) yet provided life when looked upon. Similarly, Christ became sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21), bore our curse (Galatians 3:13), and died our death so that we might receive His life. The serpent lifted on a pole foreshadows Christ lifted on a cross.

- **The Danger of Symbols Becoming Idols**: Even the bronze serpent, a God-ordained symbol that saved thousands, eventually became an idol. This warns that anything—religious practices, spiritual experiences, even sound doctrines—can become idols if they replace direct worship of God. We must continually examine whether we're worshiping God or worshiping our worship.

- **God's Timing and Promise-Keeping**: Forty years after promising Canaan, God begins fulfilling His word. The victories over Sihon and Og demonstrate that God's delays don't mean God's denials. He brings His purposes to pass in His perfect timing. What He promises, He performs, even if we don't live to see it (as with the exodus generation).

- **Family Conflicts and Lasting Consequences**: Edom's hostility toward Israel traces back to Jacob and Esau's conflict centuries earlier. Unresolved bitterness perpetuates through generations. This underscores the importance of forgiveness, reconciliation, and breaking cycles of hostility. What we leave unresolved, our children may inherit.

- **Leadership Transitions**: Aaron's death and Eleazar's succession show God's work continuing beyond any individual leader. While leaders change, God's purposes remain constant. The priesthood didn't end with Aaron but continued through his son, ultimately pointing to Christ, whose priesthood is eternal and unchanging.

## Study Questions

1. **Edom's Refusal**: Israel treated Edom as family and sought peaceful passage, yet Edom responded with hostility. How do you respond when those you expect to help refuse or oppose you? How should recognition of God's sovereignty affect your response to human rejection?

2. **Just Look?**: The cure for snakebites seemed too simple—just look at the bronze serpent. Why do humans resist simple salvation by faith? What "works" are you tempted to add to faith in Christ to feel more secure in your salvation?

3. **Complaining in the Wilderness**: Even after decades of God's provision, Israel complained about "this worthless food" (manna). What current blessings do you take for granted? How does cultivating gratitude combat the spirit of complaint?

4. **Christ Lifted Up**: Jesus said He must be "lifted up" (crucified) so that believers might have eternal life (John 3:14-15). How does understanding Christ's death as taking your judgment change how you view the cross? How does this deepen your appreciation for God's love?

5. **From Symbol to Idol**: The bronze serpent saved lives yet later became an idol requiring destruction. What good things in your spiritual life (Bible study, worship practices, theological knowledge) might be becoming substitutes for direct relationship with God?

6. **Cannot Serve Two Masters**: Paul warns that we cannot participate in both the Lord's table and the table of demons (1 Corinthians 10:21). What modern "tables" compete for Christians' spiritual loyalty? How can you examine whether your participation in cultural activities compromises your wholehearted devotion to Christ?
