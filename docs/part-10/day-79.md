# Day 79: When Curses Become Blessings

⏱️ *Estimated reading time: 33-35 minutes*

<ProgressTracker dayNumber={79} />

## Chronological Setting

Israel now camps on the **plains of Moab** across the Jordan River from Jericho, poised to enter the Promised Land. Nearly forty years have passed since leaving Egypt. The old generation has died; a new census counts the conquest generation. But before Israel crosses the Jordan, they face a unique spiritual attack: **Balak**, king of Moab, hires the prophet **Balaam** to curse Israel. What follows is one of Scripture's most remarkable accounts—God turning intended curses into powerful blessings and revealing His sovereign protection over His people.

Yet immediately after this spiritual victory, Israel falls into devastating moral and spiritual compromise at **Baal of Peor**, demonstrating that external enemies are often less dangerous than internal corruption.

## Historical Context

**Numbers 22-24** records the famous **Balaam narrative**. As Israel camped on Moab's border after defeating Sihon and Og, **King Balak** of Moab became terrified. He sent messengers with diviner's fees to **Balaam son of Beor**, a renowned prophet in Mesopotamia, hiring him to curse Israel: "For I know that he whom you bless is blessed, and he whom you curse is cursed" (Numbers 22:6).

Balaam's character is complex and troubling. Though not an Israelite, he apparently had genuine prophetic abilities and some knowledge of **Yahweh** (the LORD). When Moabite officials first approached him, he consulted God, who clearly said, "You shall not go with them. You shall not curse the people, for they are blessed" (Numbers 22:12). Balaam sent them away.

But Balak persisted, sending more prestigious officials with promises of greater rewards. Balaam asked God again, revealing his heart: he wanted God's permission to accept the lucrative offer. God permitted him to go but warned that he could only speak what God commanded. This represents **permissive will** versus **perfect will**—God sometimes allows us to pursue what we desire, even when it's not His best for us.

On the journey, God's **angel blocked the path**. Balaam's donkey saw the angel and tried to avoid it three times. Balaam beat the donkey, frustrated by its refusal to proceed. Then **the donkey spoke**, asking why Balaam beat her (one of only two talking animals in Scripture—the serpent in Eden being the other). Balaam's eyes were opened to see the angel, who rebuked him: "The way is perverse before me... the donkey saw me and turned aside before me these three times. If she had not turned aside from me, surely just now I would have killed you and let her live" (Numbers 22:32-33).

Balaam proceeded to Moab, where Balak took him to various high places to view Israel and curse them. **Four times Balak arranged sacrifices and expected curses**; four times Balaam blessed Israel instead:

- **First Oracle** (Numbers 23:7-10): "How can I curse whom God has not cursed?... Who can count the dust of Jacob?"
- **Second Oracle** (Numbers 23:18-24): "God is not man, that he should lie... He has not beheld misfortune in Jacob... The LORD their God is with them."
- **Third Oracle** (Numbers 24:3-9): "How lovely are your tents, O Jacob!... Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you."
- **Fourth Oracle** (Numbers 24:15-19): **"A star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel"**—a messianic prophecy.

Balak's fury grew with each blessing. He finally dismissed Balaam without payment, but the damage (to Balak's plans) was done: **God had turned intended curses into powerful blessings**. Israel remained blessed whether they knew about Balaam's attempts or not.

**Numbers 25** records a tragic contrast. Unable to curse Israel overtly, Balaam apparently advised Moab to **corrupt Israel morally** (revealed in Numbers 31:16 and Revelation 2:14). **Moabite women invited Israelite men to sacrifices for their gods**. Israel "yoked themselves to Baal of Peor"—engaging in both sexual immorality and idolatry. God's anger burned against Israel. A plague broke out, eventually killing 24,000.

**Phinehas**, Aaron's grandson and Eleazar's son, witnessed an Israelite man brazenly bringing a Midianite woman into the camp during the crisis. Phinehas followed them into the tent and killed both with a spear, an act of **zealous judgment** that stopped the plague. God commended Phinehas's zeal and granted him a **covenant of perpetual priesthood**.

**Numbers 26** records the **second census** (the first was in Numbers 1). Not one man counted in the first census (except Caleb and Joshua) remained alive—God's word had been fulfilled exactly. The new count prepared for the land distribution and demonstrated God's faithfulness despite forty years of wandering.

## Redemptive Significance

The **Balaam account** demonstrates **God's sovereign protection** of His chosen people. Balak hired a prophet to curse Israel, but God controlled the words that came from Balaam's mouth. This reveals a crucial truth: **no weapon formed against God's people can ultimately prosper when they are walking in His purposes**. Human opposition, spiritual opposition, even prophetic opposition cannot thwart God's plans.

Balaam's **inability to curse whom God has blessed** finds ultimate expression in Romans 8:31-34: "If God is for us, who can be against us?... Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies. Who is to condemn? Christ Jesus is the one who died—more than that, who was raised—who is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us."

The **fourth oracle's messianic prophecy**—"a star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel"—points directly to Christ. The wise men's search for the one "born king of the Jews" when they saw "his star" (Matthew 2:2) may reflect awareness of this prophecy. Jesus is the ultimate fulfillment: the King from Jacob's line who crushes God's enemies and rules forever.

Yet Balaam becomes Scripture's prime example of **prophetic corruption**. Though he couldn't curse Israel directly, he apparently showed Balak how to corrupt them through seduction (Numbers 31:16; Revelation 2:14). This reveals a sobering truth: **spiritual gifting doesn't guarantee spiritual integrity**. Balaam had genuine prophetic ability yet loved "gain from wrongdoing" (2 Peter 2:15).

The **Baal of Peor incident** demonstrates that Israel faced greater danger from moral compromise than from external enemies. Balak's armies couldn't defeat them, but Moabite women's seductions could. The pattern is timeless: sexual immorality and idolatry often intertwine, and both corrupt God's people more effectively than persecution.

**Phinehas's zeal** shows that sometimes love for God requires decisive action against sin. His was not vigilante violence but **covenant-keeping zeal** in a moment when the nation's leaders seemed paralyzed. God's commendation of Phinehas doesn't endorse violence in the church age but illustrates that true love for God includes hatred of sin and willingness to act decisively to protect His honor and His people.

## Connecting the Dots

**2 Peter 2:12-16** warns against false teachers who have "followed the way of Balaam, who loved gain from wrongdoing." Peter connects Balaam's error to greed-motivated false teaching. Like Balaam, false teachers may have genuine spiritual abilities but use them for personal profit rather than God's glory. They "count it pleasure to revel in the daytime... they entice unsteady souls... their condemnation from long ago is not idle, and their destruction is not asleep" (2 Peter 2:13-14, 3).

The warning is urgent: **spiritual giftedness doesn't equal spiritual authenticity**. Balaam could speak true prophecies yet perished in rebellion (Numbers 31:8). Modern false teachers may display charisma, eloquence, even miracles, but their love for money and pleasure reveals their hearts. The test isn't spiritual power but spiritual character and doctrinal truth.

**Revelation 2:12-17** addresses the church at **Pergamum**, commending their faithfulness despite living "where Satan's throne is" but rebuking them for tolerating **"the teaching of Balaam"**—those who teach believers they can participate in idolatry and sexual immorality without consequence. Jesus specifies: "Balaam... taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the sons of Israel, so that they might eat food sacrificed to idols and practice sexual immorality" (Revelation 2:14).

This connects to the Baal of Peor incident. Just as Balaam couldn't curse Israel but showed how to corrupt them through compromise, false teachers can't overtly destroy the church but can undermine it through promoting accommodation to culture's sexual ethics and spiritual syncretism. Jesus' call to Pergamum—**"Repent"**—remains His word to churches tolerating moral and theological compromise today.

The **promise to overcomers**—"hidden manna" and a "white stone with a new name"—contrasts with Baal of Peor's counterfeit satisfaction. While Israel sought forbidden pleasures at Peor, true satisfaction comes from Christ, the bread of life, who gives His followers intimate relationship (the secret name) and spiritual nourishment that the world cannot provide.

## Theological Threads

- **God's Sovereign Protection**: Balaam's attempted curses couldn't touch Israel because God had blessed them. This illustrates that believers are protected by God's sovereign will. When we're in His purposes, opposition may come but cannot ultimately prevail. "He who touches you touches the apple of his eye" (Zechariah 2:8).

- **The Power of God's Word**: God controlled what came from Balaam's mouth. Four times Balaam intended to curse and four times blessed instead, demonstrating that God's word accomplishes His purposes regardless of the messenger's intentions. This truth encourages us: God's word won't return void (Isaiah 55:11).

- **External vs. Internal Threats**: Moab's army couldn't defeat Israel, but Moabite women's seductions succeeded. The principle is constant: the church faces greater danger from internal compromise than external persecution. Satan, unable to destroy through opposition, often succeeds through accommodation.

- **The Wages of Unrighteousness**: Balaam represents those who use spiritual gifts for personal gain. His story warns that knowing God's will and having spiritual abilities doesn't save if the heart pursues "gain from wrongdoing." Many who prophesied and did miracles will hear Jesus say, "I never knew you" (Matthew 7:22-23).

- **Zeal for God's Honor**: Phinehas's action stopped the plague and earned God's commendation. While we don't imitate his specific violent response (belonging to the old covenant theocracy), the principle remains: love for God includes hatred of sin and willingness to take decisive stands for His honor, even when costly or unpopular.

- **Messianic Prophecy**: Balaam's oracle about the star and scepter from Jacob demonstrates that God can use even unwilling or corrupt prophets to proclaim His purposes. The prophecy's fulfillment in Christ shows the Bible's unified testimony: even Numbers points to Jesus.

## Study Questions

1. **Balaam's Dilemma**: Balaam knew God's will (don't curse Israel) yet kept asking, hoping for permission to accept Balak's offer. When have you repeatedly sought God's approval for something you already knew was outside His will? What does this reveal about your heart?

2. **The Talking Donkey**: God used a donkey to rebuke a prophet. What does this say about God's ability to speak through unexpected sources? How does this humble human pride and remind us that God doesn't need our eloquence or intelligence to accomplish His purposes?

3. **Curses to Blessings**: God turned Balaam's intended curses into blessings for Israel. How does this encourage you when facing opposition or spiritual attack? What does Romans 8:28—"all things work together for good"—mean in light of the Balaam story?

4. **Internal vs. External Threats**: Israel survived Balaam's curses but fell to sexual temptation and idolatry at Baal of Peor. What "Baal of Peor" temptations does modern culture present? Why is moral compromise often more dangerous to believers than overt persecution?

5. **Gifting vs. Character**: Balaam had genuine prophetic abilities yet loved money and led others into sin. How can churches today discern between spiritually gifted but corrupt leaders versus those with both gifts and godly character? What should you look for in spiritual leaders?

6. **Balaam's Teaching Today**: Jesus warned Pergamum about tolerating those who teach Balaam's doctrine—compromise with idolatry and immorality. Where do you see churches today accommodating culture's sexual ethics or religious pluralism? How should believers respond to such teaching?
