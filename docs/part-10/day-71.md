# Day 71: The Glory of God and the Need of Man

⏱️ *Estimated reading time: 28-30 minutes*

<ProgressTracker dayNumber={71} />

## Chronological Setting

We stand at a pivotal transition. Israel has received God's law at Sinai, established the tabernacle, and learned what holiness requires. Now they prepare to journey through the wilderness toward the Promised Land. Before we follow their travels through Numbers, we pause to reflect on two unchanging realities that will shape everything ahead: **God's infinite glory** and **humanity's profound need**. These truths, revealed in creation, inscribed in the law, and ultimately embodied in Christ, form the foundation for understanding both Israel's journey and our own.

## Historical Context

**Psalm 19** celebrates two forms of divine revelation: **general revelation** (God's glory displayed in creation, verses 1-6) and **special revelation** (God's will expressed in His law, verses 7-14). The heavens declare God's glory wordlessly yet powerfully, while the law reveals God's character specifically and personally. David recognizes that both creation and Scripture point to the same God and should produce worship and obedience.

**Psalm 90**, attributed to Moses, contrasts God's eternality with human mortality. Written during or after the wilderness wandering, it reflects on the consequences of sin (the generation that died in the wilderness) while affirming God as humanity's eternal dwelling place. Moses understands that human life, though brief and marred by sin, gains meaning only in relationship with the eternal God.

**Romans 3:9-26** represents Paul's climactic statement of the human condition and God's solution. After proving both Jews and Gentiles guilty before God, Paul declares the universal verdict: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (3:23). Yet in the same breath, he proclaims justification through faith in Christ's atoning sacrifice—God's righteousness revealed in dealing justly with sin while justifying sinners.

**Hebrews 2:5-18** explains the stunning paradox of the incarnation. Though made "a little lower than the angels" in His humanity, Christ's purpose was to taste death for everyone, thereby destroying death's power and delivering those enslaved by fear. His full identification with humanity equips Him to be a merciful and faithful high priest.

## Redemptive Significance

These passages together reveal the **gospel's necessity** and **magnificence**. God's glory—displayed in creation's grandeur and law's perfection—establishes a standard humanity cannot meet. We fall short not occasionally but universally; we fail not partially but completely. The law, far from saving us, exposes our desperate condition.

Yet God's glory also guarantees His solution. The same God whose majesty fills the cosmos and whose holiness demands perfection has acted in Christ to provide what we could never achieve. Christ's incarnation—becoming "a little lower than the angels"—represents the infinite condescending to the finite, the holy One identifying with the sinful, not to compromise His glory but to display it in redemption.

The **two-directional movement** in these texts is crucial: God's glory descends to us (in creation's revelation, the law's instruction, and supremely in Christ's incarnation), while we are lifted up to God (through justification, sanctification, and ultimately glorification). We cannot ascend to God's glory on our own; we can only receive the glory He brings down to us in Christ.

## Connecting the Dots

Psalm 19's celebration of God's revelation in creation and law finds its fulfillment in Christ, who is both the Word through whom creation came into being (John 1:3) and the Word who became flesh to reveal God fully (John 1:14). The law's perfection, which Psalm 19 praises, accuses us (Romans 3:20) until Christ fulfills it on our behalf (Matthew 5:17).

Psalm 90's meditation on human frailty connects directly to Romans 3's diagnosis: our sinfulness isn't merely a matter of wrong choices but reflects our fundamental condition as descendants of Adam, mortal and rebellious. Yet Hebrews 2 reveals that Christ entered this very frailty, becoming fully human, subject to death, to destroy death from within and bring "many sons to glory" (Hebrews 2:10).

The **glory we've lost** (Romans 3:23) and the **glory we're destined for** (Romans 8:18-30) bracket our current existence. We live between these two glories, justified by Christ's work, being transformed into His image, awaiting final glorification. The wilderness journey Israel is about to undertake mirrors our own—moving from slavery through a dangerous passage toward promised rest, sustained not by our strength but by God's faithfulness.

## Theological Threads

- **God's Glory**: God's infinite greatness, displayed in creation and law, establishes both His right to reign and the standard for holiness. This glory doesn't diminish when He stoops to save us but shines brighter in redemption than in creation.

- **Universal Sinfulness**: All humanity, without exception, has sinned and falls short of God's glory. This isn't merely breaking rules but missing the purpose for which we were created—to image God's glory. Sin is cosmic treason and personal failure.

- **Justification by Faith**: God declares sinners righteous not by lowering the standard or overlooking sin but by providing a righteous substitute. Christ's death satisfies divine justice while extending divine mercy—God is both "just and the justifier" (Romans 3:26).

- **Christ's Identification**: Jesus didn't merely appear human but became fully human, experiencing temptation, suffering, and death. This complete identification qualifies Him to represent us, sympathize with us, and save us.

- **Law's Purpose**: The law reveals both God's character and human sinfulness. It cannot save but serves to drive us to the One who can. In Christ, we're freed from the law's condemnation while empowered to fulfill its righteousness through the Spirit.

- **From Frailty to Glory**: Our current weakness, mortality, and sinfulness aren't the final word. Through Christ, frail humans are being transformed into God's glorious image, destined for eternal life in resurrected bodies.

## Study Questions

1. **Two Books**: Psalm 19 describes two "books"—creation and Scripture—through which God reveals Himself. How do you see God's glory in each? Why are both necessary?

2. **Human Condition**: Psalm 90 emphasizes human mortality and frailty. How does recognizing your finitude and sinfulness affect your relationship with God? Does it drive you to despair or to hope?

3. **No Distinction**: Romans 3:22-23 states there's "no distinction" between Jews and Gentiles in sinfulness or in salvation. How does this universal diagnosis and universal solution shape your view of other people?

4. **God's Justice**: Romans 3:25-26 says God put forward Christ "to show his righteousness" because He had passed over former sins. How does the cross demonstrate both God's justice and His mercy?

5. **Christ's Humanity**: Hebrews 2 emphasizes that Jesus had to be "made like his brothers in every respect" to save us. Why was Christ's full humanity necessary? How does it comfort you in your struggles?

6. **Glory Lost, Glory Gained**: We've fallen short of God's glory (Romans 3:23), yet Christ brings "many sons to glory" (Hebrews 2:10). How does understanding both your lost glory and your promised glory shape how you live today?
